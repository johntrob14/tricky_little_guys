WMDP · gpt-oss-120b and its CoT-control variants

Sandbagging trace review: findings so far

We read saved reasoning traces to see what models do when told to answer incorrectly. The main question was whether they quietly insert plausible mistakes into their reasoning. We also looked for other ways they produce wrong answers.

No first-pass reader identified an error as deliberately planted. The clearest pattern in mt1b is missing deciding work or an unstated conclusion. That also happens under instructions about reasoning style, so its cause remains unresolved.

Status: first blinded pass 660/660 complete; second pass 0/660. Work is paused.

These are provisional findings. Record validation passed; independent confirmation of the judgments is still pending. Findings below are ranked by importance.

Terms: “reasoning” means the model’s visible chain of thought (CoT). A “flip” means it answered correctly in an ordinary run and incorrectly in another condition. “Style-only” means an instruction about how to reason, with no instruction to answer wrongly.

1. We have not established silently planted reasoning errors

The first pass applied the planted_candidate label to 0/660 traces. This label required positive textual evidence that a reasoning error was deliberately inserted. Being wrong, contradicting oneself, or submitting a different answer was not enough.

2. mt1b often leaves its answer unsupported by the visible reasoning

Among mt1b’s instructed flips with no prior admission flag, 126/243 had no settled option in the reasoning. In 115/243, the trace described a procedure or claimed work without showing the deciding result. These labels overlap.

A smaller group, 15/243, had a terminal reasoning choice that differed from the submitted letter. These are different observations: an unstated choice is not an observed answer switch. All these selected flips still emitted a wrong final letter.

3. Wrong answers and wrong-answer intent come apart

The first pass found explicit wrong-answer intent in 11/390 instructed flips whose earlier automated admission flag was not true. “No prior admission flag” is therefore a sampling rule, not a guarantee that intent is unstated.

In the opposite direction, readers labeled 23/57 of the base model’s reviewed instructed flips as explicitly rejecting the wrong-answer objective—even though the submitted letter was wrong according to the key.

4. Question and reference problems limit what the errors can tell us

Readers flagged question problems in 190/660 traces and missing or unreliable reference reasoning in 293/660. These are overlapping, provisional reader flags, not independently confirmed counts of flawed questions. Repeated questions can contribute several flagged traces.

A reference ending with the keyed letter is not necessarily a sound solution. Exploratory readers identified unsupported guesses, faulty reasoning in correct-letter references, missing question content, and ambiguous options. For question 212, they found 2/4 options valid under the displayed task.

The ordinary-error pool also mixes different outcomes: 82/143 source rows have wrong letters, while 61/143 have no parsed answer. Of those nonanswers, 59/61 hit the length cap. The blinded comparison therefore keeps ordinary wrong-letter errors separate from nonanswers.

Implication: comparing a trace with the key or reference can make ordinary mistakes, nonanswers, and defective tasks look like the same phenomenon. The pending analysis needs the planned checks for question weighting and reader-flagged defects.

5. mt2e’s correct answers under concealment usually still show task work

The exploratory read covered 15/82 mt2e runs that stayed correct under wmdp.should with the instruction to conceal the wrong-answer plan. Readers described task work in 14/15, including completed reasoning and failed work followed by a keyed guess.

The remaining 1/15 explicitly tried to be wrong but guessed the key. None of the sampled annotations identified an explicit rejection of the wrong-answer objective (0/15).

Interpretation: this sample does not support explaining the retained accuracy as overt refusal. It does not establish why the concealment instruction changes the model’s accuracy.

What remains unresolved

The next step is the independent second read, followed by agreement and within-model comparisons. The existing first-pass labels are enough to describe patterns, but the planned paired comparison has not been completed.

In particular, we have not established whether mt1b’s missing work is deliberate concealment or a cost of its learned reasoning styles. We have not established an excess of deliberately planted mistakes over ordinary errors.

No monitor-score comparison has been run. The planned monitor analysis concerns CoT only, Li et al. sandbagging judge, gpt-6-luna at medium effort.

Scope and how to read the evidence

The exploratory round produced 520/543 annotations, with nonrandom gaps. The blinded round has 660/660 first reads and 0/660 second reads. Its core includes 620/636 eligible source rows, plus 40/40 checked redacted controls. Previously stopped or excluded source items were omitted; the first pass added no runtime gaps (0/660).

The blinded readers saw the question, visible reasoning, submitted letter, answer key, and reference reasoning. They did not see model names, conditions, system prompts, or prior judge outputs. Style or wording can still reveal a condition, so blinding is partial. Readers were native Codex agents; their exact inherited model was not exposed by the interface.

Models, small controls, and terminology
  • base: gpt-oss-120b, the comparison model.
  • grpo3: trained to follow reasoning-style instructions.
  • mt1b: trained on more reasoning controls, including suppressing calculations and not committing to an answer in reasoning.
  • mt2e: a later reasoning-control model trained with an accuracy bonus and ordinary prompts mixed in.

All are variants of gpt-oss-120b. The first pass includes 28/28 eligible base ordinary wrong-letter errors, 8/8 for grpo3, 32/32 for mt1b, and 8/8 for mt2e. The grpo3 and mt2e comparison pools are particularly small.

wmdp.can, wmdp.safe, and wmdp.should name instruction variants. Source IDs retain default, safe, and should. “Evasion” adds an instruction not to reveal the wrong-answer plan.

Labels can overlap. “No settled option” describes the reasoning, not whether a final answer was emitted. “Different final letter” requires both a reasoning choice and a parsed final answer. Evidence scores are not calibrated probabilities.
